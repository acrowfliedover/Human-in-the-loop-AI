Krippendorff's alpha for the Module 7 visitor-intent labels
Date: 2026-11-08

Source: Module_7_assignment/labeled_visitor_intent.csv
Codebook used when reading disagreements:
  Module_7_assignment/visitor_intent_classification_worker_instructions.md

This file reports one reliability coefficient for the five annotators, shows the
arithmetic, and explains why Krippendorff's alpha is the coefficient that fits
this table. Cohen's kappa and Fleiss's kappa are computed on the same codes so
the comparison is numerical as well as conceptual.


1. What was measured

30 visitor messages (V1–V30).
5 annotators (annotator_1 through annotator_5).
4 unordered categories: Buyer, Seller, Renter, Other.
Every cell is filled. There are 150 codes and no missing ratings.

The reliability sample is those five columns only. final_label is a derived
label (majority, with one tie broken on V22). adjudicated only marks that tie.
Putting final_label into the rater matrix would count the same judgments twice.

The distance is nominal. Two codes agree only when they are the same word.
Buyer, Seller, Renter, and Other have no rank order, so an ordinal or interval
distance would invent closeness the codebook does not define.

CSV spellings (Buyer, Seller, Renter, Other) are the same four categories as
the codebook's lowercase labels.


2. Formula

Krippendorff's alpha (nominal), from the coincidence matrix in Krippendorff
(2011), "Computing Krippendorff's Alpha-Reliability":

    alpha = 1 - (D_o / D_e)

For each item u, n_u is the number of codes on that item (here n_u = 5 for
every item). n_uc is how many of those codes are category c.

Observed coincidences, summed over items:

    o_cc = sum over u of  n_uc * (n_uc - 1) / (n_u - 1)
    o_ck = sum over u of  n_uc * n_uk / (n_u - 1)     when c ≠ k

n = sum of every o_ck. With complete data, n equals the number of codes, 150.
n_c is the c row sum. n_c is also the number of times category c was used.

Nominal distance: delta_ck = 0 if c = k, and 1 otherwise.

    D_o = (n - sum_c o_cc) / n
    D_e = (n^2 - sum_c n_c^2) / (n * (n - 1))

D_o is the observed disagreement. D_e is the disagreement expected if the
recorded values were paired by chance. alpha = 1 is perfect agreement.
alpha = 0 matches chance. alpha < 0 is worse than chance.

Krippendorff's cutoffs, which belong to this coefficient:
  alpha >= 0.800   customarily required before treating the labels as reliable
  alpha >= 0.667   lowest level at which tentative conclusions are still arguable
  alpha <  0.667   the labels are not reliable enough to rely on

5. Observed disagreement, expected disagreement, and alpha

    D_o = 43 / 150 = 0.286666...

    sum of n_c^2 = 87^2 + 9^2 + 19^2 + 35^2
                 = 7569 + 81 + 361 + 1225
                 = 9236

    n^2 - sum of n_c^2 = 22500 - 9236 = 13264
    n(n - 1)           = 150 × 149    = 22350

    D_e = 13264 / 22350 = 0.593467...

    D_o / D_e = (43/150) / (13264/22350)
              = 43 × 149 / 13264
              = 6407 / 13264

    alpha = 1 - 6407/13264
          = 6857/13264
          = 0.516963...

Rounded to three decimals, alpha = 0.517.

Observed agreement, read off the same matrix, is 107/150 = 0.713.
About 71% of pairable codes match. Chance agreement on this skewed label
distribution is 1 - D_e = 0.407. Removing that chance agreement is what
takes 0.713 down to 0.517. Buyer alone is 58% of the codes, so two codes
drawn at random match more often than they would under a balanced scheme.

The 43 disagreement units split by unordered pair:

    Buyer–Other     16    37.2%     (8 + 8 in the two off-diagonal cells)
    Buyer–Renter    10    23.3%
    Renter–Other    10    23.3%
    Seller–Other     7    16.3%
    Buyer–Seller     0
    Seller–Renter    0

No item contains both a Buyer code and a Seller code, or both a Seller code
and a Renter code. The unsettled boundary is whether a message is a
transaction at all, and whether a housing plan is a purchase or a lease.


6. Uncertainty from having only 30 items

Items were resampled with replacement (10,000 bootstrap samples, seed
20261108). Each sample recomputes nominal alpha on 30 drawn items.
Annotators are held fixed.

    bootstrap mean                         0.505
    95% percentile interval                0.346 to 0.660

The interval is wide because 30 items is a small reliability sample.
The upper end, 0.660, is still below 0.667. Under this resample, the data
do not reach Krippendorff's tentative cutoff.


7. The same table scored with Cohen's kappa and Fleiss's kappa

Cohen's kappa needs exactly two raters. Five raters produce C(5,2) = 10
separate kappas. For raters A and B, with N = 30:

    p_o = share of items where A and B wrote the same label
    p_e = sum over categories of (A's share of c) × (B's share of c)
    kappa = (p_o - p_e) / (1 - p_e)

    Pair                    p_o     p_e     kappa
    annotator_1, annotator_2   0.667   0.424   0.421
    annotator_1, annotator_3   0.833   0.443   0.701
    annotator_1, annotator_4   0.767   0.443   0.581
    annotator_1, annotator_5   0.767   0.433   0.588
    annotator_2, annotator_3   0.600   0.353   0.381
    annotator_2, annotator_4   0.567   0.353   0.330
    annotator_2, annotator_5   0.733   0.416   0.544
    annotator_3, annotator_4   0.800   0.420   0.655
    annotator_3, annotator_5   0.700   0.377   0.519
    annotator_4, annotator_5   0.700   0.377   0.519

    Mean of the 10 kappas = 0.524
    Lowest = 0.330 (annotator_2 with annotator_4)
    Highest = 0.701 (annotator_1 with annotator_3)

Fleiss's kappa uses all five raters, with n = 5 fixed on every item.
P_i is the agreement within item i, P_bar is the average of the P_i, and
p_j is the overall share of category j:

    P_i = (sum_j n_ij^2 - n) / (n (n - 1))
    P_bar = 0.713333...     which equals 107/150, and also equals the
                            average of the 10 pairwise raw agreement rates
    p_j = 87/150, 9/150, 19/150, 35/150
    P_e = sum p_j^2 = 0.410489
    Fleiss kappa = (P_bar - P_e) / (1 - P_e) = 0.514

Side by side, three decimals:

    Krippendorff's alpha          0.517
    Fleiss's kappa                0.514
    Mean of 10 Cohen's kappas     0.524

On this particular file the three summaries sit near each other. The file
is complete, every item has the same five raters, and the distance is
nominal, which is the special case where Fleiss's kappa and nominal alpha
almost meet. The gap is the chance model: Fleiss uses sum of p_j^2, while
alpha uses n_c through the finite-sample denominator n(n-1). With 150
codes that correction is worth about 0.003 on the coefficient.

The mean Cohen kappa is also near 0.52, but that average hides a spread
from 0.330 to 0.701. A report that quoted only annotator_1 with annotator_3
would call the study substantial. A report that quoted only annotator_2
with annotator_4 would call it weak. Alpha is one coefficient for the
whole matrix, so it does not depend on which pair is shown.


8. Why alpha is the better metric for this annotation

Cohen's kappa is a two-rater coefficient.
Five annotators do not have one Cohen's kappa. Averaging the 10 pairwise
kappas gives a descriptive number, and section 7 shows that number, but it
is not the multi-rater estimator. Each pair has its own chance term, built
from that pair's margins, and a coder who is in four pairs is counted four
times. Cohen's kappa also drops an item, or requires an imputation rule, as
soon as one of the two raters skips it. Weighted kappa can express an
ordered distance, and it still covers only two raters.

Fleiss's kappa does accept more than two raters, and it can be computed
here because every item has exactly five codes. That completeness is a
property of this CSV, not a property a visitor-intent study can count on.
The ordinary Fleiss formula assumes the same number of ratings on every
item. One skipped message forces a choice: drop the item, or leave the
formula. Fleiss also treats the ratings on an item as an unordered pool.
It has no term for which person produced which code, so the coefficient
cannot be left as-is when the design is "these five named annotators."
A further naming problem: when the rater count is two, Fleiss's kappa is
not Cohen's kappa. The shared word "kappa" does not mean the two formulas
agree. Fleiss is also a nominal formula. Moving the labels to an ordered
scale, or deciding that Buyer-versus-Renter is a smaller miss than
Buyer-versus-Seller, requires a different coefficient.

Alpha is defined for any number of raters from two upward, and for items
that were coded by different subsets of those raters. A unit with a single
code adds nothing. A unit with two or more codes enters with weight n_u,
through the (n_u - 1) pairing. The same alpha, with a different delta, covers
nominal, ordinal, interval, and ratio data. This study uses the nominal
delta, which matches an unordered four-way label. The chance correction is
built from the same coincidence counts as the observed disagreement, with
the n(n-1) correction, so observed and expected disagreement are comparable
quantities. The published reliability cutoffs above are stated for alpha.
Landis-and-Koch bands ("moderate," "substantial") were written for Cohen's
kappa and are not the scale used here.

Artstein and Poesio (2008), "Inter-Coder Agreement for Computational
Linguistics," make the same recommendation for annotation: prefer alpha
when the number of coders can vary, when some codes are missing, or when
the study needs one coefficient that still works if the scale type changes.
Hayes and Krippendorff (2007) give the same reason for content analysis.

This CSV happens to be a favorable case for Fleiss (complete, fixed five
raters, nominal labels), and the two coefficients differ only in the third
decimal. That closeness does not make them interchangeable on the next
batch. A Module 10 form in which a worker skips an item, or in which a
sixth category is later treated as ordered, is inside alpha's definition
and outside classical Fleiss and Cohen.


9. What the annotators disagreed about

Leave-one-annotator-out alpha, still nominal, four raters, 30 items:

    without annotator_1    0.483
    without annotator_2    0.591
    without annotator_3    0.491
    without annotator_4    0.519
    without annotator_5    0.500

Removing annotator_2, who used Other on 13 of 30 items, raises alpha from
0.517 to 0.591. That is the largest single-coder effect, and 0.591 is still
below 0.667. The disagreement is shared. Annotator_2 accounts for much of
the Buyer–Other mass (V2, V3, V7, V20, V26, and the second Other on several
mixed items) and does not account for all of it. Annotator_5 supplies the
lone Other on V9 and the lone Renter on V14.

The disagreements sit on boundaries the Module 7 codebook already tried to
close.

Buyer versus Other. The codebook routes house-hunting, a second home, and
"moving" or "relocating" without the word rent to buyer. The 4–1 and 3–2
Buyer/Other items are mostly that boundary: looking at listings or a second
home (V2), a home in Kissimmee with no verb buy (V3), room for parents (V7),
looking at houses on a visit (V20), and a fenced yard with no verb buy (V26).
V9 is the explicit buying message that still drew one Other.

Buyer versus Renter, and Renter versus Other. The codebook says a purchase
that will later be rented to guests is buyer, a historical-rent question
about a house the visitor may buy is buyer, and rent-now-then-buy is renter
when the lease is the near-term plan. The labels do not follow that
consistently. V14 (buy a short-term rental) is 4 Buyer and 1 Renter. V29
(use a townhome part of the year and rent it out) is 3 Buyer and 2 Renter.
V16 (historical rental rates on a Reunion house) is 3 Renter and 2 Other,
and no annotator chose Buyer, which is the label rule 3 of the codebook
names. V17 (a house, and also rent before buying) is 3 Renter, 1 Buyer,
1 Other, so the majority matches the rent-first rule while two coders do
not. V6 (property management only) is the codebook's other example; the
codes are 3 Other, 1 Buyer, 1 Renter.

Seller versus Other. Seller never collides with Buyer or Renter on the same
item. The Seller conflicts are messages the codebook puts in other: a
company check (V4, one Seller), a name with no housing plan (V15, one
Seller), and a request for an email (V21, two Sellers). V1, the explicit
sale of a Florida home, is unanimous Seller.

V22 is the adjudication. "Looking to move" to a named area, with no lease
and no buy verb, is the codebook's usual buyer case. The five codes are
Buyer, Other, Renter, Renter, Other. final_label = Other is the tie break,
not a majority.

V27 (algae and air quality) and V28 (the single word Hi) are the clean
other cases. V27 still drew one Renter. V28 is unanimous.

Read together: raw agreement looks acceptable because Buyer is common and
the obvious messages (explicit buy, explicit sell, explicit lease, a bare
greeting) are unanimous. Chance correction, and the item-level table, show
that the coders are not interchangeable on the messages the codebook had to
spell out. Those are the messages a later gold label will get wrong if it
trusts a majority vote without a second pass.


10. Result

Nominal Krippendorff's alpha = 6857/13264 = 0.517.
95% item-bootstrap interval = 0.346 to 0.660.
Fleiss's kappa on the same codes = 0.514.
The 10 Cohen kappas range from 0.330 to 0.701 (mean 0.524).

By Krippendorff's standard the five-annotator labels are below the line for
tentative use (0.667) and well below the line for reliable data (0.800).
The bootstrap interval stays under 0.667. Majority labels will look cleaner
than this coefficient because 23 of 30 items already have a 4-or-5 majority.
That majority is not a substitute for reliability on the seven split items,
or on the Buyer/Other and Buyer/Renter boundary the other items only barely
cleared.

Alpha is the reported coefficient because it is the one multi-rater,
missing-data-capable, scale-general statistic among the three, and because
a single Cohen kappa does not exist for five annotators. On this complete
nominal table it and Fleiss's kappa nearly match. The methodological
difference shows up as soon as a rater skips an item or the distance stops
being nominal.
